Stream-WAM

Streaming Your World-Action Model for Real-Time Robot Manipulation.

Stream-WAM introduces action-conditioned streaming for world-action models. It overlaps world-action inference with robot execution and feeds the committed action prefix back into future-video generation, so the model imagines what comes next with knowledge of the motion already underway. The robot keeps acting while its next world-action chunk is prepared.

Abstract

World-action models jointly generate visual futures and robot action chunks, giving manipulation policies a shared representation of what may happen and how the robot should act. Their iterative video-action generation, however, makes real-time deployment difficult: synchronous execution exposes inference latency as robot idle time, while naive asynchronous switching can create disagreement between consecutive chunks.

Stream-WAM provides a unified framework for studying streaming strategies in WAM-based robot control. Building on this framework, our method feeds the committed action prefix back into the world model: the action currently being executed conditions future-video generation while the next world-action chunk is predicted asynchronously. This couples ongoing execution to the visual future imagined by the model instead of treating inference–execution overlap as scheduling alone. We evaluate the approach on LIBERO, RoboTwin 2.0, and RoboCasa.

Why capable world-action models still make robots wait.

World-action models (WAMs) offer something richer than a policy that maps the latest image directly to a motor command. By generating future video together with future actions, a WAM can represent both what the scene may look like and what the robot should do to bring that future about. The visual prediction makes the intended evolution of a manipulation task explicit, while an action chunk gives the controller a temporally structured plan rather than a sequence of isolated reflexes.

That joint prediction is especially attractive for long-horizon manipulation. A generated future can carry information about object motion, contact, and progress toward a goal across several control steps. The action stream is therefore grounded in an imagined trajectory of the scene, and the two outputs can be trained as parts of the same generative process.

The same design is expensive at deployment time. Producing video and actions through iterative generation is substantially heavier than emitting a single low-dimensional command. On a real robot, the relevant delay is not just one reported model kernel: it includes image capture, preprocessing and transfer, world-action inference, command dispatch, and the interpolation needed to execute a chunk safely.

In a conventional synchronous loop, all of that work sits between observation and motion. The robot finishes one chunk, waits for the next world-action prediction, and only then moves again. If the scene or the robot changes during this pause, the next command also begins from information that is already aging.

The result is visible without a profiler. A manipulation that should be continuous becomes stop-and-go: the arm advances, freezes while the next chunk is generated, and advances again. Larger action chunks can amortize some inference calls, but they also keep the robot committed to an older plan for longer. Faster kernels help, yet the basic schedule still turns model latency into robot idle time.

This is the practical reason for streaming. The goal is not merely to lower an offline latency number, but to reorganize the loop so that useful prediction happens during useful motion. The robot should continue executing the plan it already has while the model thinks ahead about the chunk that comes next.

Asynchrony removes the wait, but exposes a boundary.

The direct way to hide inference latency is to overlap it with execution. Once a current action chunk begins, the model starts preparing the next one instead of waiting for the robot to finish. When inference fits inside the execution window, compute is moved off the critical path and the controller can approach continuous motion even when generation itself is not instantaneous.

But overlap changes the information available at a handoff. The next chunk is predicted from an earlier observation while the current chunk is actively moving the arm and changing the scene. Two neighboring predictions may therefore disagree over the portion of time they both describe. Switching from one independently generated chunk to the next can produce a jump in position, velocity, or direction—the characteristic boundary discontinuity of naive asynchronous execution.

This is partly a temporal-alignment problem. The controller must determine how far execution will have advanced by the time a new prediction becomes usable and then select the matching suffix of that prediction. Alignment prevents the system from replaying commands for moments that have already passed. It does not, by itself, guarantee that two independently generated trajectories agree where they meet.

Existing work explores several ways to repair that disagreement. World Action Models in Real Time studies inference-time real-time chunking (RTC), which guides the new sample toward the trajectory already being executed, often by adding a consistency objective during iterative generation. This can impose continuity without retraining the base model, but it adds work to the inference procedure and its effectiveness depends on whether the guidance can reliably constrain the committed region.

That empirical study compares naive overlap, blending, inference-time RTC, and prefix-conditioned generation in a common setting. On that particular platform, the authors report that their RTC velocity guidance did not reliably constrain the delayed region, whereas prefix-conditioned methods provided the strongest overall precision–smoothness trade-off. Those findings are platform-specific rather than a universal verdict, but they make an important point: continuity is a modeling constraint, not a scheduling detail.

Prefix-conditioned generation addresses the boundary more directly by treating the committed action prefix as known context and generating a continuation after it. It requires the model to be trained for that conditioning pattern, but it does not add the same guidance loop at inference time. For action continuity, this is a strong and principled baseline—not something Stream-WAM is meant to dismiss.

A WAM, however, predicts more than actions. If the action continuation is conditioned on the executed prefix while future video is still generated without explicitly knowing what motion is underway, the two output streams are conditioned on different accounts of the same interval. This is the gap isolated by our Stream-WAM w/o Action Conditioning row: it retains prefix-conditioned action continuation, but removes the executed action from future-video generation. The remaining question is therefore WAM-specific: can the world prediction itself be made aware of the action that is already changing the world?

Stream-WAM conditions the visual future on the action already underway.

Stream-WAM couples execution and generation at the model level. During asynchronous rollout, the robot continues to execute the current chunk, and the committed action prefix is fed back into the world model. That prefix records the intervention being applied between the observation that launched inference and the future state for which the next chunk is needed.

The key design choice is where this information goes. Stream-WAM uses the executing prefix to condition future-video generation, so the predicted visual trajectory reflects the motion already in progress. If the gripper is closing, the arm is approaching an object, or a contact is being maintained, the generated future is asked to remain consistent with that action context rather than extrapolating from the initial image alone.

The next action chunk is then generated together with this action-conditioned visual future. In other words, the model does not first imagine an action-agnostic scene and attach a continuation afterward. Its future video and future action are produced from a shared account of what the robot has already committed to doing.

This turns inference–execution overlap from a systems optimization into a coupled streaming process. The scheduler determines when the next prediction begins; temporal alignment determines which part of it belongs after the handoff; and action conditioning determines what future the WAM should imagine while the current action is being executed. Each component solves a different part of the same control problem.

The loop repeats at every boundary. A prepared chunk becomes the current command, the robot begins executing it, and its committed prefix becomes context for the following prediction. When inference remains within the available action window, the model thinks ahead without asking the robot to stop; when the scene changes unexpectedly, the method still depends on fresh observations and replanning rather than assuming that an old prefix is always sufficient.

Stream-WAM is also the research framework used to separate these effects. It places synchronous WAM execution, pure asynchronous overlap, inference-time continuity methods, prefix-conditioned action generation, and action-conditioned world-action streaming behind a common evaluation interface. This makes it possible to compare task success, chunk latency, and end-to-end control time instead of treating any one of them as a complete measure of streaming quality.

The current study carries that comparison across LIBERO, RoboCasa, and RoboTwin, using FastWAM-Joint, X-WAM, and StarWAM model families as its respective foundations. The backbones and protocols differ, but the central test remains the same: can a WAM prepare its next decision while the robot acts, without losing the relationship between the action underway and the visual future it predicts?

Task performance. To evaluate Stream-WAM across different World Action Model families, we begin by further training FastWAM-Joint with our streaming approach on LIBERO. We then extend the same design to X-WAM on RoboCasa and StarWAM on RoboTwin 2.0 to examine its applicability across benchmarks and model families. Our evaluations use four NVIDIA H100 GPUs.

We compare Stream-WAM with general purpose robot policies and World Action Model baselines in task performance and inference efficiency. CD refers to one-step consistency distillation. We also conduct ablation studies on Stream-WAM by removing action conditioning or the slot encoder to evaluate the contribution of each component. Inference efficiency. Task success alone does not reveal how much inference interrupts robot execution. We therefore measure chunk latency, the time required to prepare the next action chunk, together with end to end episode time, which captures the accumulated cost of inference, execution, and replanning over a complete rollout.

Stream-WAM reduces chunk latency by 12.0× on LIBERO relative to FastWAM, 4.0× on RoboTwin 2.0 relative to StarWAM-Joint, and 3.2× on RoboCasa relative to X-WAM. These latency gains yield end to end speedups of 3.0× and 2.6× on long and short LIBERO tasks, 1.4× on RoboTwin 2.0, and 1.8× on RoboCasa.

Stream-WAM reframes streaming as a relationship between execution and prediction, not only as a way to schedule the same computation earlier.

The first provisional conclusion is that action conditioning provides a direct link between the robot motion underway and the visual future used for its next decision. It gives asynchronous prediction a model-level account of what execution is doing during inference.

The second is that streaming performance needs to be read jointly with control success. Chunk time, end-to-end time, and task outcomes describe different parts of the system; none alone establishes that a robot can act continuously and successfully.

The third is about the scope of this preview. The technical report will carry the broader analysis, limitations, and failure cases. Until then, this page presents the method motivation, released artifacts, evaluation protocols, and current tables without filling those missing sections with speculative claims.
